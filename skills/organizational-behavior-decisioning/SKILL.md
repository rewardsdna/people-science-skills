# Organizational Behavior Decisioning Skill

**Builds on:** `people-science-core-reasoning`

Use this skill together with the Core Reasoning skill whenever the decision or analysis involves workplace behavior, culture, leadership, motivation, teams, norms, power, change, or organizational systems.

## Purpose

Help the agent reason rigorously about organizational behavior by:

- Distinguishing fundamental mechanisms from inherited assumptions
- Preferring observable behavior over abstract labels
- Identifying multiple plausible behavioral explanations
- Evaluating interventions for their actual behavioral consequences
- Avoiding over-psychologizing of workplace phenomena

This is not a textbook of OB theories. It is a decisioning layer that forces clearer thinking about *why* people behave as they do in organizations.

## Core Questions for Any OB Analysis

Always start with the Core Reasoning sequence, then focus on these OB-specific questions:

1. **What is the observable behavior?**
   - Move from constructs (“low engagement”, “poor culture”, “lack of motivation”) to concrete actions or patterns.

2. **What mechanisms could produce this behavior?**
   - Incentives, constraints, information, social norms, identity, skill, opportunity, power, measurement systems, etc.

3. **Which explanations are assumptions vs. evidence-based?**
   - Classify each proposed mechanism using the Core Reasoning evidence framework.

4. **What alternative explanations exist?**
   - Force consideration of at least one credible competing account before accepting a preferred story.

5. **What would change if we intervened?**
   - Anticipate both intended and unintended behavioral responses.

## Key Distinctions

| Concept | Clarification |
|---------|---------------|
| Construct | Abstract label (engagement, culture, psychological safety) |
| Observable behavior | What people actually do or fail to do |
| Mechanism | The process that produces the behavior |
| Assumption | An untested or weakly supported belief about the mechanism |
| Situational factor | Features of the environment, system, or structure |
| Individual factor | Traits, skills, preferences, or history of the person |
| Norm | Shared expectations that shape behavior |
| Incentive | Anything that systematically rewards or punishes behavior |

Never treat a construct as an explanation. Always ask what behavior it is meant to represent and what produces that behavior.

## Common Assumptions to Challenge

- “People are demotivated.”
- “We have a culture problem.”
- “Employees resist change.”
- “Managers need more soft skills.”
- “Psychological safety is low.”
- “Engagement drives performance.”
- “People leave because of their manager.”
- “We need stronger values.”
- “Teamwork is broken.”

Treat each as a hypothesis. Demand mechanisms and evidence.

## Behavioral Explanation Checklist

When explaining workplace behavior, consider:

- **Incentives** — What is actually rewarded or punished?
- **Constraints** — What limits options or makes some behaviors costly?
- **Information** — What do people know or believe?
- **Social influence** — Norms, peers, status, identity
- **Job design** — Autonomy, variety, feedback, significance, skill use
- **Measurement** — What is tracked and therefore optimized?
- **Power and politics** — Who controls resources or outcomes?
- **History and path dependence** — Prior experiences and sunk costs
- **Selection** — Who is in the role or organization in the first place?

Prefer situational and systemic explanations before leaping to individual dispositions.

## Guardrails Against Over-Psychologizing

- Do not diagnose individuals from organizational data.
- Do not reduce complex patterns to a single trait or “type.”
- Distinguish temporary states from stable characteristics.
- Consider that people adapt strategically to the systems they are in.
- Stated preferences and survey responses are data points, not necessarily the underlying mechanism.
- Avoid deterministic claims about human nature.

## Intervention Analysis

Before recommending any change aimed at behavior:

1. Specify the target behavior in observable terms.
2. State the proposed mechanism of change.
3. Identify what else the intervention might encourage or discourage.
4. Consider who benefits and who may be disadvantaged.
5. Ask how people might respond strategically.
6. Define how success (and side effects) will be observed.

## Decision Output Format

When analyzing an OB issue or recommending action:

1. **Decision or question being addressed**
2. **Observable behavior of interest**
3. **Proposed mechanism(s)**
4. **Evidence status** of each key claim
5. **Alternative explanations**
6. **Contextual factors that matter**
7. **Likely consequences of intervention** (intended and unintended)
8. **What should be tested or monitored**
9. **Repeatable principle or decision rule** (if applicable)

## Integration with Core Reasoning

Always run the full Core Reasoning sequence first.  
This skill specializes the Behavior, First Principles, Alternatives, and Trade-offs stages for organizational phenomena.

## Guiding Principle

Behavior is produced by systems and situations as much as by individuals.  
Explain it that way.
